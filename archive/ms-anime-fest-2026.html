Jackson Showed Up: Mississippi Anime Fest 2026 Delivers a Weekend Worth the Wait

March 15, 2026 • Mississippi Trade Mart, Jackson • By Staff Reporter

Two days of voice actors, cosplay craftsmanship, and the kind of community energy that reminds you why regional conventions matter.

The doors of the Mississippi Trade Mart closed on Mississippi Anime Fest 2026 on Sunday afternoon, and by any reasonable measure, the convention delivered. Over two packed days — Saturday from 10 a.m. to 6 p.m. and Sunday from 11 a.m. to 5 p.m. — thousands of attendees moved through the Trade Mart's 110,000 square feet of column-free exhibition space for what organizers at VXV Events LLC have quietly built into the Gulf South's most serious regional pop culture event.

The crowd was multigenerational in a way that few conventions manage without feeling forced. Parents in casual clothes trailed children in elaborate Naruto and My Hero Academia costumes. College-aged groups navigated the vendor hall with the focused urgency of people who arrived with lists. And here and there, older fans — the ones who remember watching Futurama in real time — stood patiently in line at Billy West's table, phones out, grinning.

"This is the kind of event that makes you proud to be from Mississippi."

The Voice Actor Floor Was the Heartbeat of the Event

The celebrity guest lineup for 2026 was the strongest in the festival's history. Billy West, Roger Craig Smith, Stephanie Sheh, Justin Cook, and more than a dozen others held court in the autograph hall across both days, and the energy around those tables never really dropped. West, who voiced Fry, Professor Farnsworth, and Zoidberg on Futurama, drew lines that stretched deep into the vendor section by mid-morning Saturday. Roger Craig Smith — the current voice of Sonic the Hedgehog — was equally mobbed, particularly by younger attendees who came specifically for him.

What made the guest hall work was its layout. With enough space between tables to move without being crushed, and ATMs positioned near the entrance to handle the cash-only culture of convention autographs, the organizers avoided the bottlenecks that plague similar events in tighter venues. Fans who planned ahead got what they came for. Those who didn't plan early enough for the Craftsmanship prejudging slots found that lesson waiting for them at the Briumbra Photography booth by early afternoon.

The Cosplay Competition Set a New Standard

Saturday's costume contest, staged on the main floor at 5 p.m., was the emotional peak of the weekend. The three-division structure — Youth, Masquerade, and Craftsmanship — gave the competition a range that suited the crowd. The Youth division was what it should be: pure enthusiasm, zero pressure, and the kind of parental pride that fills a room. The Masquerade brought out performers who understood that wearing a costume well is its own discipline.

But the Craftsmanship division was something else. Judged by featured cosplayers Little Red Fox Cosplay, 13.Katz Cosplay, and Gamma Rae Cosplay — all of whom ran prejudging sessions earlier in the afternoon — the division showcased work that would hold up at any national convention. Entrants were assessed on eight technical criteria including construction quality, material choices, wig work, prop fabrication, and fit. The contestants who brought Build Books documenting their process in photographs made a visible impression on the judges. Several of the Novice-level entries were, frankly, better than some Advanced work at comparable events in larger markets.

Beyond the Main Stage

The vendor and Artist Alley sections hummed consistently throughout both days. The 2026 event enforced a ban on AI-generated artwork in the creator section — a policy that drew quiet but genuine appreciation from the artists who spent months making the work on their tables. The tabletop gaming area and video game tournament stations ran with the kind of low-key intensity that gaming communities generate naturally, with Jackson State University's E-Sports contingent among the organized groups competing across the weekend.

Fan-organized panels filled the programming schedule alongside the Q&A sessions with celebrity guests, and the 501st Rancor Raiders Garrison brought the Star Wars corner of the floor to life with the commitment that fan groups reliably deliver. The cosplay meetups organized by specific fandoms — particularly the Attack on Titan and Jujutsu Kaisen gatherings — were impromptu enough to feel genuine and structured enough to actually happen.

"The convention's ban on AI-generated art in the creator section drew quiet but genuine appreciation from artists who spent months making the work on their tables."

What Mississippi Anime Fest Gets Right

Regional conventions live or die on atmosphere, and Mississippi Anime Fest has figured out something that larger events sometimes lose: the ratio of people to space. The Trade Mart's 110,000 square feet absorbed the crowd without ever feeling claustrophobic. Parking — approximately 1,500 spaces on-site — was not the ordeal it becomes at conventions held in urban convention centers with multi-block walks. Security at the entrance was thorough without being hostile. The peace-bonding process for prop weapons moved efficiently.

None of that happens by accident. VXV Events has been running this festival long enough to know where the friction points are and how to smooth them. The result is a convention that feels professionally run without losing the grassroots warmth that makes these gatherings worth attending in the first place.

Mississippi Anime Fest 2026 is over. The wristbands are off, the vendors are packed up, and the Trade Mart has already returned to its normal configuration. But the conversations happening right now — in parking lots, in group chats, in cosplay forums — are already pointing toward next year. That's the best review a regional convention can get.
